Floors and rooms

Floors are energy levels (the 2 in 2p). Each floor has room types: s is 1 room, p is 3 rooms, d is 5 rooms. Every room fits 2 tenants. That's why s holds 2, p holds 6 and d holds 10. Nothing to memorize: it's rooms × 2.

The superscript

The small raised number in 2p⁶ is a head count: how many tenants live in that room type. Add them all up and you get the total tenants, which is the atomic number.

Why the periodic table is the order

Each element has one more tenant than the one before. Walking across a row is moving tenants in one at a time, and the block tells you which room type the newest one took. Row 4: 2 s columns, 10 d columns, 6 p columns = 4s, 3d, 4p.

Average atomic mass: a weighted grade

If 75% of your grade is 35 and 25% is 37, you end up near 35, not 36. Most chlorine atoms weigh 35, so the average is 35.45. Mass × percent as a decimal, for each, then add.

1. Writing configurations about 4 points, 8 min

What you're writing. An electron configuration lists where every electron in the atom goes. Each piece, like 2p⁶, has three parts:

  • 2 is the energy level (the row of the periodic table)
  • p is the type of spot: s, p or d
  • ⁶ is the superscript, the small raised number. It's how many electrons are in that spot.

How many each one holds. s holds 2, p holds 6, d holds 10. So 2s² is fine, but 2s⁶ is impossible.

The order you fill them in (the same every time):

The bold ones in the box below are where you went wrong on mock test B. You wrote 2s⁶ where it's 2p⁶, 4p where it's 3p, and 3d is the one that comes after 4s even though the number goes down.

How to do one, step by step:

  1. Find the atomic number on the periodic table. That's how many electrons you have to place.
  2. Go through the order above, filling each spot all the way, and keep a running total.
  3. When the next full spot would go past your number, put only the leftover electrons there and stop.
  4. Check: add up all the superscripts. They must equal the atomic number.

Silicon (14): 1s² (total 2), 2s² (4), 2p⁶ (10), 3s² (12), then 3p would be 18, which is too many. 14 − 12 = 2 left, so 3p². Answer: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². Check: 2+2+6+2+2 = 14.

Manganese (25): fill up to 4s² (total 20). 3d holds 10 but you only have 25 − 20 = 5 left, so 3d⁵. Answer: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁵.

Why "the periodic table is the order". Each row of the table is one energy level. The two left columns are s spots, the six right columns are p spots, and the ten middle columns (row 4 and down) are d spots. Walk across a row left to right and you're walking through the filling order. Row 4 goes 4s, then the middle block (that's 3d, one number lower), then 4p. So you can read the order straight off the table instead of memorizing it.

Noble gas form is a shortcut. [Ar] stands for the first 18 electrons (1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶), so you start writing at 4s. Mn = [Ar] 4s² 3d⁵.

The order to know: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p

2. Average atomic mass 2 points, 5 min

Why it's an average at all. Real chlorine is a mix of two isotopes: some atoms weigh 35 (34.969) and some weigh 37 (36.966). The mass on the periodic table (35.45) is the average weight of one atom from that mix.

Picture 100 chlorine atoms. About 76 of them weigh 34.969 and about 24 weigh 36.966 (because the percents are 75.78% and 24.22%).

  • Weight of the 76 light ones: 34.969 × 75.78 = 2650
  • Weight of the 24 heavy ones: 36.966 × 24.22 = 895
  • All 100 together: 2650 + 895 = 3545
  • One atom on average: 3545 ÷ 100 = 35.45

Dividing by 100 at the end is the same as turning each percent into a decimal at the start. That's the whole method:

For each isotope: mass × (percent ÷ 100). Then add them. (34.969 × 0.7578) + (36.966 × 0.2422) = 26.50 + 8.95 = 35.45

Check yourself: the answer has to land between the masses, closest to the most common one. 35.45 is between 35 and 37, closer to 35 because 76% of the atoms are 35.

Three isotopes? Same thing, just three products to add.

Copper and chromium: the only two exceptions 3 points

This has nothing to do with charge. It's about the normal, neutral atom. Only two elements break the filling order on your test: Cr and Cu. Every other element follows the order.

Cr (24)Cu (29)
What the order predicts[Ar] 4s² 3d⁴[Ar] 4s² 3d⁹
The d sublevel is4 of 5 orbitals, one short of half full9 of 10, one short of full
So one 4s electron moves to 3d[Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵ (half full)[Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ (full)

Why: a d sublevel that is exactly half full (one electron in each of its 5 orbitals) or completely full (10) is extra stable, so the atom gives up one 4s electron to get there. Count check: 4s¹ 3d⁵ is still 6 electrons after [Ar], 18 + 6 = 24.

Charge is a separate idea. That's ions: a positive ion loses its 4s electrons first (Co²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁷). If a test ever asks for Cu⁺, start from the exception and remove the single 4s: Cu⁺ = [Ar] 3d¹⁰.

Write these two word for word 4 points

4s fills first, leaves first: "4s fills before 3d because 4s is lower in energy. But 4s is farther from the nucleus, so when an ion forms, its electrons are removed first."

Copper: "Copper is [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰ instead of 4s² 3d⁹ because one 4s electron moves into 3d. A completely full d subshell is more stable."

3. Three small facts about 1.5 points, 1 min

Alpha particles are positive (2 protons and 2 neutrons, no electrons). The nucleus is also positive, and like charges push each other away. That's why the few alpha particles that came close to the tiny nucleus bounced back. On your mock test you called them negative: a negative particle would be pulled in, not bounced back, so the charge is what makes the story work.

Dates: Thomson found the electron in 1897 (the plum pudding model came in 1904: negative electrons scattered in a positive sphere). Bohr's model is 1913.
